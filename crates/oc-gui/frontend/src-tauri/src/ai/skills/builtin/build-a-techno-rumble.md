Define the rumble as a separate low rhythmic texture under a dry kick.
Keep the kick attack intact and one owner for its low body. A large reverb
on the master is not an isolated rumble layer. Inspect routing first.

## Build a return or a retained print
Send the kick to a dedicated wet path using route-groups-and-returns.
Smear with a supported delay/reverb, shape the spectrum and control its
envelope. Query actual device parameters; do not assume a named vendor chain.
If separate routing is unavailable, print an isolated wet passage and use
that audio on its own track. Preserve the dry source and document the print.
Hear wet only to confirm there is no accidental second dry kick.

Choose low-frequency weight by listening with the original kick and bass.
The useful cutoff depends on the source and arrangement, not a fixed value.
Compare before and after any saturation: it can add mids back above a filter.
Keep low texture centered when width weakens mono playback; verify the sum.
Do not interpret correlation alone as a successful low-end check.

## Recover into the next kick
Use a verified kick-keyed compressor or supported volume automation for
space. Set depth by the overlap, then recovery by the offbeat pulse.
Hear whether ducking releases in time for the texture to speak without
covering the next attack. A static EQ cut cannot fix late envelope recovery.
Test the final hit of a phrase and the first hit after a break, including
state and tails; a steady loop alone can hide a bad return entrance.

Compare dry kick, wet path and the sum at matched listening levels. Read
align-layered-transients if the body weakens. Do not move the intentional
delayed rumble onto the kick attack merely to maximize a waveform peak.
Audition the full bass passage and a transition; remove a redundant bass layer
before adding more processing. Retain only the chosen bounded print/tail.
